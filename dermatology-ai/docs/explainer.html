Ablation lab illustrative only

Toggle sources to see the shape of the argument: dermoscopic-only data looks strong in training and brittle in clinic; adding clinical photographs and dark-skin data costs headline accuracy and buys the thing that matters. The percentages are invented to make that point and are labelled as invented on every bar.

Why it is not a real ablation: there is no trained model to ablate, and no evaluation report to quote. A real ablation needs a frozen checkpoint, one variable changed at a time, and identical splits.

Concept codex

Sixteen ideas the project stands on. Each card has four faces: what it is, how this project does it, what it is for, and a toy you can poke. Toy outputs are demonstrations.

What it can do

  • Sort a photographed lesion into routine, follow-up or urgent — it does not name a disease.
  • Show the concepts and the pixel regions behind a lane, so a clinician can audit the reason.
  • Record a clinician’s override, with a reason, in an audit trail.
  • Report per-skin-tone behaviour when a frozen checkpoint exists — with sample sizes and intervals.

What it cannot do

  • Replace a dermatologist or produce a diagnosis.
  • Recognise a condition it never saw — including Kaposi sarcoma, which is paused for lack of a cohort.
  • Be trusted on skin tones it was not validated on; no African cohort is in hand.
  • Work on a bad photograph: blur, shadow, a lesion at the frame edge.

Break the AI

Take the same lesion and damage it. Watch the evidence collapse — and note that the honest output is “uncertain, refer”, not a confident wrong answer.

This desk runs on a synthetic prediction set generated in your browser, so the arithmetic is real and the model is not. Where the project’s own numbers belong, the cell says not yet measured — the correct value until a frozen checkpoint and an evaluation report exist.
